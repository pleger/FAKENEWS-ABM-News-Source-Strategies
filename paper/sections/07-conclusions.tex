\section{Conclusions}

This study used an open, survey-informed ABM to evaluate how malicious news sources can imitate trusted-source attributes and how platform-controllable reach interacts with those strategies. Across 2,010 seeded runs, source camouflage consistently improved audience-related outcomes, with combined imitation producing the largest gain. Campaign timing and duration primarily determined how long the changed source could compete for attention. Reach restrictions monotonically reduced strategy effects and eliminated them when direct reach was zero. Truth-sensitive social recommendations attenuated false-repost effects rather than bypassing reach controls.

The sensitivity experiment provides the qualification needed for policy interpretation. The conclusion that camouflage can attract users was stable across most memory and connectivity assumptions. The conclusion that it increases total false reposts was conditional on memory persistence. Consequently, policy evaluation should not equate malicious-source popularity with misinformation exposure, should use both source- and publication-level measures, and should test recommendations and reach governance jointly.

The model is a transparent computational laboratory, not a digital twin of X. Its value lies in controlled comparison, explicit assumptions and reproducible uncertainty analysis. External validation with observed X data is the next step required before predictive or population-level claims can be made.
